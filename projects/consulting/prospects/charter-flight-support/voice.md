# Charter Flight Support Voice

Status: v0.1 draft from public sources only, created 2026-05-14. This should be refined with CFS-approved emails, sales decks, support templates, newsletter examples, and direct team feedback.

Use this as the first voice anchor for CFS content, sales, education, and AI-assisted drafting.

## Voice thesis

Charter Flight Support sounds like the calm operator in the room when aviation gets messy.

The voice is practical, steady, and industry-literate. It understands the pressure of a mechanical event without dramatizing it. It protects three things at once: the passenger experience, the broker/operator relationship, and the business economics of recovery.

The brand should feel like:

- Experienced, not corporate.
- Calm, not casual about risk.
- Clear, not simplistic.
- Helpful, not salesy.
- Relationship-driven, not fluffy.

## Audience

Primary audience:

- Part 135 charter brokers
- Charter operators
- Private aviation teams responsible for client experience, sourcing, and recovery

What they care about:

- Protecting client relationships
- Avoiding ugly last-minute cost exposure
- Recovering trips quickly and professionally
- Respecting operator preferences and DNU lists
- Keeping their own brand front-and-center
- Knowing exactly what is and is not covered before pressure hits

What they do not need:

- Generic luxury travel language
- Vague peace-of-mind claims
- Overexplained aviation basics
- Panic-driven sales copy
- AI-ish thought leadership fluff

## Core promise

When an aircraft goes mechanical, CFS goes to work.

Supporting messages:

- We source recovery options fast.
- We help handle the cost difference.
- Your client experience stays protected.
- Your team stays focused on client communication.
- Your operation's standards, preferences, and DNU list are respected.
- CFS is a support service with a membership model, not insurance.

## Registers

### 1. Operational register

Use for website pages, FAQ, sales collateral, onboarding, pricing, support qualification, AOG process, and member communication.

Traits:

- Short, direct sentences.
- Clear definitions.
- Specific boundaries.
- Financial clarity.
- No hype.
- No jokes.
- No dramatic crisis language.

Example:

> If the aircraft goes mechanical, notify CFS immediately. Our team begins sourcing recovery options 24/7. You lead client communication while we handle recovery logistics.

### 2. Community register

Use for LinkedIn, event copy, partner shoutouts, industry networking, and light newsletter intros.

Traits:

- Warmer and more conversational.
- Peer-to-peer.
- Industry-insider without being cliquey.
- Allows casual phrases like "stoked," "friends," "come find us," and "hope to see you there."
- Still anchored in aviation outcomes.

Example:

> We're heading to SDC next week. No booth, just good conversations. If you want to talk AOG recovery, broker/operator workflows, or how to protect client relationships when a trip goes sideways, send us a note.

### 3. Thought leadership register

Use for The Fix, podcast recaps, data insights, event panels, and educational posts.

Traits:

- Starts with a real industry tension.
- Uses data carefully.
- Avoids pretending one metric explains everything.
- Makes operators and brokers feel seen.
- Ends with a practical implication.

Example:

> Aircraft type matters, but it is not the whole reliability story. Operator performance, fleet model, seasonality, and recovery readiness all change the outcome. The smarter question is not "Will this aircraft go mechanical?" It is "What happens if it does?"

## Words and patterns to avoid

Avoid:

- "luxury redefined"
- "elevate your journey"
- "white glove" unless CFS uses it in approved material
- "revolutionary"
- "game-changing"
- "unlock"
- "disrupt"
- "frictionless" unless describing a specific process
- "guaranteed" unless legally approved
- "insured" or "insurance" for CFS support
- "we operate the aircraft"
- "we control the flight"
- "risk-free"
- "never worry again"

Avoid emotional overreach:

- Do not make the broker/operator sound helpless.
- Do not shame teams for not having support.
- Do not dramatize AOG events as disasters.
- Do not overpromise that every recovery will be perfect.

## Style rules

1. Lead with the operational reality.
   - Good: "A mechanical creates pressure on cost, sourcing, and client trust."
   - Weak: "Travel should always feel effortless."

2. Explain the business consequence.
   - Good: "When the best recovery option costs $30,000 more, teams are forced into a tradeoff."
   - Weak: "Unexpected costs are frustrating."

3. Keep the client relationship in view.
   - Good: "The aircraft changed. The client experience does not have to."

4. Use concrete process language.
   - Good: "Send the confirmed operator quote with dates, route, and aircraft type."
   - Weak: "Submit your details and we will take it from there."

5. Name boundaries clearly.
   - Good: "Scheduled maintenance is not an AOG."
   - Weak: "Some situations may not qualify."

6. Prefer confident restraint over hype.
   - Good: "CFS works best when we know your operation before pressure hits."
   - Weak: "CFS is the ultimate solution for every charter challenge."

## Channel guidance

### Website

Use the operational register. Every section should answer one of these:

- What happens?
- Who does what?
- What does CFS cover?
- What does CFS not cover?
- Why does this matter to the broker/operator?
- What should the reader do next?

### FAQ

Be direct and boundary-heavy. Use "yes/no/if" structures where possible.

Good shape:

> Yes, if a tail has been assigned and then goes AOG, CFS support applies. If the operator returns the trip before tail assignment, support does not apply.

### LinkedIn

Use the community register. Pair warmth with a real business reason.

Good shape:

> Great few days at Charter Connect. The best conversations were not abstract. They were about the real decisions brokers and operators have to make when a trip goes mechanical: protect the client, control the cost, and keep everyone moving.

### The Fix

Use the thought leadership register. Make it practical and specific. Avoid generic trend commentary.

Good shape:

> This month, we are looking at recovery readiness: the difference between having access to options and having a plan for choosing the right one under pressure.

### Sales outreach

Lead with the operational pain, not the product.

Good shape:

> When a recovery option is available on time but costs significantly more, the decision gets complicated fast. CFS helps brokers and operators protect the client experience without absorbing the full replacement cost delta.

### AOG support communication

Be calm, concise, and procedural. No flair. No speculation. No unsupported reassurance.

Good shape:

> We are sourcing recovery options now. We will prioritize your preferred operators and respect the DNU list on file. Please send the current client timeline and any operator tech log documentation as soon as available.

> Never worry about AOGs again.

## AI drafting rules

When using AI to draft as CFS:

1. Identify the channel first: website, FAQ, LinkedIn, newsletter, sales email, event recap, AOG communication.
2. Use the matching register.
3. Keep "not insurance" clear where pricing, reimbursement, or support eligibility is discussed.
4. Do not invent support terms, percentages, limits, operators, recovery outcomes, or client names.
5. Do not make legal, safety, or guaranteed-availability claims without approved source language.
6. Do not send or publish AI-drafted AOG, sales, pricing, or client-facing communications without human review.
7. Prefer one specific operational insight over three broad claims.
8. If writing for LinkedIn, allow warmth. If writing during an active AOG, remove warmth and write procedurally.

## One-paragraph target voice

When a charter aircraft goes mechanical, the problem is bigger than sourcing another plane. The team has to protect the client relationship, make a fast recovery decision, and manage a cost difference that can change the economics of the trip. CFS exists for that moment. We source recovery options, help handle the cost difference, and work as an extension of your team so the client experiences continuity, not chaos.
